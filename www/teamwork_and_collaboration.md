# Project Management and Teamwork Statement

This statement explains how the SolarSafe project was managed as a team, reflects on which strategies worked and which did not, and draws recommendations for future projects.

## Teamwork Strategy

At the outset, the team divided responsibilities according to individual strengths. James, with mechanical and aerodynamic experience, took the role of project lead and shared the mechanical design work. Josh acted as lead communicator with Benard in Kenya and led the experimental data analysis, while Zac took ownership of the electronics. This subsystem ownership maps directly onto the three individual reports on this site, which made accountability simple: each member always knew which deliverables were theirs, but all members contributed to all deliverables through discussion and research.

Progress was managed against a milestone timeline — defining scope, proposing concepts, testing to find the best viable solution, developing that concept, and finally evaluating effectiveness at the product level. A risk assessment, cost estimate, and a set of contingency plans were prepared up front, and a shared running document recorded decisions so the whole team stayed aligned between meetings.

## What Worked

Clear ownership combined with frequent communication proved highly effective. When the departmental risk assessment approval stalled, the team deliberately progressed other workstreams such as design iteration, component selection, and software, so that no one was idle waiting for paperwork. Sourcing power supplies showed the same strength: James updated the risk assessment for the new power requirements, Josh liaised with the Dyson Centre technicians, and Zac worked with the EIETL, solving in parallel a problem that would have bottlenecked any one person.

Contingency planning also paid off. When two Peltier modules were destroyed during testing, the pre-planning meant the team could solve the problem and implement replacement devices quickly.

When Zac had to deal with a situation away from Cambridge, the rest of the team worked to progress all elements such that time was not lost. Communication was key in this case, ensuring that work could be resumed and progress continued.

## What Did Not Work

The biggest external failure was underestimating departmental processes. Risk assessment approval and purchasing through approved vendors took far longer than planned, and some components could only be obtained with technician help or personal purchases. The knock-on effect was a compressed testing window at the end of the project: repeat experiments and a wider range of ambient conditions would have strengthened the results, but there was no schedule float left to accommodate them.

The team identified early that all three members are perfectionists, inclined to design the most performant solution immediately rather than improve incrementally. Despite recognising this, early design phases still ran long, and prototypes were occasionally over-worked before testing could validate the underlying concept. Had this not been the case, more time early-on could have been dedicated to departmental processes, leading to more testing time later in the project.

## Recommendations

Two constructive lessons emerge:

- Firstly, administrative processes: risk assessments, ordering, and facility approvals, should be started at project kick-off and treated as critical activities, not formalities. 

- Secondly, a team of perfectionists benefits from explicitly committing to incremental prototyping: build the simplest version that can be tested, and let data justify refinement. We got a little too excited about the full technical solution too early.

Overall, the strategy of strength-based roles, parallel workstreams, and planned contingencies served the project well, and with earlier administrative work, the team would have converted its strong proof-of-concept into more validated results.
